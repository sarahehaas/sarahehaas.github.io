Designing trust into Autofill

Translating a product-strategy finding into clearer save and use experiences—and a stronger baseline for every experiment that followed.

Business goal

Grow adoption of Autofill across mobile checkout flows without increasing trust, compliance, or revenue risk.

My contribution

Simplified save and use experiences, strengthened trust signals, preserved legal coverage, and created reusable disclosure guidance.

Results

46% fewer disclosure words, 58% shorter consent text, 0% regression in the save experiment, and a 1.6% purchase-completion lift in a use-flow experiment.

This work grew out of a broader product audit that identified security and privacy concerns as Autofill’s leading adoption barrier. Read the strategy audit that led to this work →

The challenge

Autofill already delivered clear value by making future checkout faster. But to grow adoption, the opt-in moment needed to work harder: people were being asked to save payment information mid-purchase, with dense legal language and limited trust context.

The audit and supporting research made the problem clearer. Convenience was not enough. People needed to understand who they were trusting, what would be saved, how it would be protected, and how they could stay in control.

  • Meta was not naturally perceived as a payments brand, which raised the trust bar.
  • Users wanted specific security reassurance—not just shorter copy.
  • Added visual or interaction complexity could hurt conversion, so the experience needed to become simpler, not more explanatory.
  • Legal and privacy rationale was scattered across prior reviews, making every new experiment harder to approve and scale.

My role

I led content design for Autofill save and use flows, partnering with product design, UXR, product, engineering, legal, and privacy. I translated research into content strategy, redesigned the strings and disclosure hierarchy, created experiment variants, and built a reusable source of truth that could support future work across apps, platforms, markets, and eligibility states.

Approach

01

Understand the why before changing a word

I reconstructed the legal, privacy, and product rationale behind the existing experience instead of treating approved language as an untouchable artifact.

02

Ground decisions in research

UXR findings shaped the hierarchy: lead with specific security reassurance, make the value exchange visible, and avoid adding complexity.

03

Give people agency

I replaced passive disclosure framing with direct language about what people were doing, what Meta would save, and where they could manage it.

04

Build for reuse and legal review

I created an approved baseline that could flex across product variants without forcing every team to reconstruct the reasoning from scratch.

Impact 1 · Content optimization

Simplifying the decision moment

The work shifted the experience from compliance-first language to clearer, trust-forward product content. It reduced cognitive load without removing legal coverage or adding unsupported promises.

Selected content decisions

Each change addressed a specific problem surfaced by research or product behavior.

Element Before After Why it changed
Save headline “Save card for next time?” “Save card for faster checkout?” Added a clear, immediate reason to opt in without overpromising.
Security message Not present “Your info will be encrypted and securely saved.” Placed specific reassurance at the moment users were deciding whether to trust Meta.
Consent footer 63-word passive legal block 26-word active disclosure Preserved legal coverage while making the action, storage, and control model easier to understand.
Use-flow headline “Confirm card to use autofill?” “Enter CVV to use saved card” Replaced a vague confirmation request with the exact next step.
Primary action “Confirm” “Use saved card” Named the action people were taking instead of asking them to confirm an unclear state.
46% fewer disclosure words across the full save flow
58% shorter consent footer with the same legal coverage
0% regression in the save experiment, establishing a cleaner baseline

Clarifying the use flow

The use flow had a different problem: people were asked to “confirm” a saved card while looking at an empty CVV field. The revised experience made the next step explicit, added better input cues, and named the action clearly.

1.6% increase in purchase completion in the use-flow experiment
4 experiment arms covered in one consolidated review cycle

Impact 2 · Reusable source of truth

A stronger baseline for every future experiment

This was not a one-off rewrite. I consolidated scattered legal, privacy, product, and content guidance into a shared framework that explained what had been approved, why it mattered, and how the same principles should apply across future variants.

  • Facebook and Instagram language across the in-app browser.
  • iOS and Android formatting and implementation differences.
  • U.S., rest-of-world, and stricter European privacy requirements.
  • Flows where CVV could be saved and flows where it could not.
  • Different eligibility states and experiment directions.

Product, design, legal, privacy, and content could align once instead of re-litigating the same decisions for every launch. Future experiments started from an approved, optimized baseline rather than a blank page.

Reflection

Content can build trust. It can’t manufacture it.

Strong content reduces confusion, supports trust, and makes the value exchange legible. But even the strongest strings cannot fully close a structural trust gap on their own. Product impact also depends on the quality, timing, and visibility of the underlying experience.

My job was to make sure the words did their part: satisfy legal requirements, give people clearer control, support experimentation, and scale across product variants without creating new risk.
