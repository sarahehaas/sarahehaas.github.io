I guide product evolution

Designing for the journeys between discovery and conversion

A portfolio of browser experiments revealed that post-click commerce was not a single linear funnel. People often moved through repeated cycles of discovery, consideration, reconsideration, and revisitation before they were ready to act. I helped turn that fragmented learning into a clearer product strategy — connecting individual experiments to the broader needs of keeping track, continuing exploration, and building confidence.

Problem: The team was testing isolated post-click ideas without a shared model for how they supported the broader decision journey. → Outcome: A clearer framework connected product experiments to recurring user needs and helped shape which capabilities should continue, stop, or evolve into Meta’s broader browser direction.

My involvement

Connecting product experiments to a broader model of user value

Role

Sole content designer across HXP workstreams and their later evolution into Enhanced Browsing and agentic-browser experiences.

Scope

Post-click browser experiences spanning product and seller information, content, prices, recommendations, revisitation, permission models, onboarding, and emerging agentic capabilities.

Contribution

Developed the decision-journey framework, shaped value propositions and experiment variants, synthesized results into product principles and investment decisions, and co-designed positioning research with a UXR partner.

The challenge

The team was testing what a browser could add after an ad click — but the work lacked a shared model of the journey it was trying to support.

HXP began as a broad exploration of how Meta’s in-app browser could create more value after someone clicked an ad. The team tested commerce and decision-support concepts involving product information, seller content, social signals, pricing, recommendations, revisitation, and other ways to help people evaluate what they encountered after leaving the ad.

The opportunity was broad, but the work was fragmented. Different concepts addressed different moments, and the traditional funnel model did not explain the behavior the team was seeing. People were not always discovering a product and moving directly to purchase. They often left, returned, compared, reconsidered, and resumed their search across multiple sessions.

Without a shared journey model, weak results were difficult to interpret. A concept might have failed because of its content, interaction design, trust model, timing, distribution, or because the underlying product idea did not create enough value.

The original concepts produced mixed outcomes. Some launched, some continued, some paused, and some stopped. HXP began as that exploration — not as a plan to build an agentic browser.

The approach

I used content, research, and journey modeling to connect individual experiments to a broader product strategy.

01

I reframed post-click commerce as a nonlinear decision journey.

The initial product framing treated the space largely as a path from discovery to conversion. But research and product behavior pointed to something more complex.

For many purchases, people repeatedly moved between discovery and consideration before they were ready to act. They revisited products, resumed searches, compared alternatives, and rebuilt context across multiple browsing sessions.

I developed a model that separated:

Reconsideration

The thinking and research involved in evaluating options and building confidence.

Revisitation

The actions involved in saving, organizing, remembering, and returning to previously interesting content.

That distinction helped identify three recurring jobs:

  • Keep track
  • Continue looking
  • Build confidence

The framework gave the team a more useful way to connect user needs to product capabilities across HXP.

02

I mapped individual products to the needs they were actually serving.

Once the journey was clearer, the portfolio became easier to interpret.

Tabs was not simply a browser utility. It helped people resume exploration and return to pages they had already opened.

Product Recommender was not simply another recommendation surface. It was an attempt to help people continue consideration by exploring alternatives from within the merchant journey.

Save and Link History supported retrieval and memory. Autofill reduced friction once people were ready to act.

This model made it possible to distinguish between products that looked related but served different user needs — and to identify where multiple concepts were competing for the same job.

03

I treated content as part of the product hypothesis.

Value propositions, information hierarchy, interaction language, and UXR stimuli helped determine whether an idea was being communicated poorly or did not create enough underlying value. Three representative experiments led to three different product decisions.

Product spotlight

Product Recommender: applying the decision-journey model to a real browser experience

Product Recommender tested whether Meta’s browser could help people continue exploring after they landed on a merchant page.

The concept added a browser-level entry point — such as “Discover similar items” — that opened a recommendation surface based on the product being viewed. People could explore alternatives, return to merchant pages, and continue through the shopping journey without losing access to the recommender.

The product question

Could the browser help someone build confidence by surfacing relevant alternatives at the moment they were already considering a product?

My contribution

  • The product framing and value proposition
  • Entry-point language
  • Title and hierarchy explorations
  • Content-design variants
  • The relationship between the recommendation surface and the merchant page
  • Feedback rounds with Content Design and cross-functional partners
  • The distinction between recommendation value and generic ad delivery

The team explored multiple ways to frame the experience, including product-led, merchant-led, personalized, and utility-led directions.

The learning

The concept produced a strong CTR increase, but broader measures did not support continued investment.

That result mattered because it separated two questions:

  • Could the entry point attract attention?
  • Did the overall product create enough value to improve the broader journey?

The answer was not the same.

The team stopped investing in that form of the product, but the work produced reusable learning about contextual recommendations, browser-level entry points, persistent product surfaces, merchant handoffs, and the difference between engagement and durable value.

04

I continued shaping the product as its permission model changed.

The permission thread evolved from multi-product consent to an Enhanced Browsing toggle and later to Enhanced Browsing with AI. I shaped permission language, value framing, and control explanations at each stage.

Each model introduced new questions about what capabilities were being combined, what the browser would do, what data it required, and what AI added. The work demanded more than disclosure — it required a coherent explanation of value, scope, control, and system behavior.

05

I helped shift launch strategy from AI-first language to concrete value and control.

Concept research I helped design with a UXR partner found that value-centered positioning significantly outperformed AI-first positioning when AI was not paired with clear explanations of data use. Control language and lightweight context improved comprehension.

Impact

A fragmented portfolio of experiments became a clearer strategy for supporting complex decision journeys.

The original HXP work produced mixed standalone results. Collectively, it:

  • Clarified that post-click commerce was not a single linear funnel
  • Established a shared model of reconsideration and revisitation
  • Connected multiple browser capabilities to recurring user needs
  • Produced a revenue-positive social-context interaction
  • Identified which commerce concepts merited further investment
  • Prevented continued investment in concepts whose broader value did not hold
  • Generated interaction, permission, and control patterns that later informed Meta’s evolving browser direction
  • Shifted positioning away from AI-first messaging toward concrete value and user control

The value was not that every experiment succeeded. The value was that the team became better able to distinguish signal from novelty, engagement from durable value, and isolated features from a coherent product system.

Where the story ends: By the time this case study ends, leadership believed the later agentic-browser product had generated enough evidence to support continued long-term investment. The later product was not a direct continuation of every HXP concept. It inherited selected learning, capabilities, interaction patterns, and strategic principles as the browser direction evolved.

Reflection

Emerging products rarely begin as coherent systems.

They evolve through partial successes, discarded implementations, reframed problems, and capabilities that find uses no one predicted.

The most valuable contribution is not forcing every experiment to succeed. It is creating the framework that helps a team understand what each result means, what deserves to continue, and how separate ideas connect to a larger direction.

“I help emerging products evolve by turning fragmented experiments into shared understanding, shared understanding into direction, and direction into experiences people can use.”
