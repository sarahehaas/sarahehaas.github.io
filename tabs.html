Designing continuity into an in-app browser

People often left Meta’s in-app browser before they were finished exploring. Returning could mean finding the original link again, retracing their steps, or losing the context entirely. I helped define what continuity should mean for the browser and turn that need into a product model, investment case, and launched Android MVP.

Problem

Returning to something from the past and continuing something unfinished are not the same problem.

Outcome

A continuity concept became a funded, launched product.

The challenge

The browser already had ways to support previously visited content.

Tabs needed to solve a different job: preserving active browsing context so people could pick up where they left off.

The team first had to make that distinction clear enough to justify building a new product.

What I did

I turned an ambiguous return problem into a product teams could build.

I helped define the user need and jobs-to-be-done, compare product models, and shape the strategic narrative used with leadership.

Then I worked through the actual experience: naming, first-use education, entry points, core flows, edge cases, accessibility-ready content specifications, and launch adaptation.

The final language made the distinction concrete:

Open Tabs

Helped people continue browsing pages that were still open.

Link History

Helped people return to links they had visited previously.

Resolution

Tabs launched as a Facebook Android MVP after earning roadmap investment and leadership alignment around continuity as a distinct browser need.

When a later technical regression made retained tabs unreliable, I partnered with Design and Engineering to reduce feature visibility rather than continue promoting behavior the product could not consistently support.

Where the story ends: The regression ultimately outweighed the feature’s value, and Tabs was sunset.

The work also established continuity as a legitimate browser problem and produced strategic and interaction models that remained useful after the interface itself disappeared.

Reflection

Good product strategy does not guarantee that a feature will survive.

It creates a clearer basis for deciding what to build, how to evaluate it, and what to carry forward when conditions change.
