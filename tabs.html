I shape strategy

Designing continuity into an in-app browser

People often left Meta’s in-app browser before they were finished exploring — and had no reliable way to resume where they left off. I helped define revisitation as a distinct browser problem, build the strategic case for investment, and shape Tabs from an early product model into a funded, launched MVP.

Problem: People regularly left the browser before completing a task, but the product had no clear model for preserving or resuming their context. → Outcome: A funded Tabs MVP launched on Facebook for Android and exceeded its engagement target by 46%, while the underlying continuity model influenced later browser strategy.

Defining the product opportunity, experience model, and launch narrative

Role

Content designer and strategic partner on the founding Tabs team.

Scope

Concept development through Facebook Android MVP, iOS testing, early Instagram exploration, and launch adaptation.

Defined the revisitation problem and jobs-to-be-done, evaluated competing product models, shaped the leadership narrative, developed naming and first-use concepts, co-designed core and edge-case flows, created accessibility-ready content specifications, and adapted the experience when technical reliability changed.

The challenge

The team needed to prove that returning to browser content was a distinct product problem — not simply another form of engagement.

Most people who entered Meta’s in-app browser from an ad did not complete a purchase or finish exploring in one visit.

They might compare products, read related information, think about a decision, or want to return later. But once they left the browser, there was no reliable, visible way to resume where they had stopped.

The problem was broader than shopping. People could leave behind articles, merchant pages, entertainment, reference content, and other links opened from Facebook.

Before proposing a feature, the team needed to answer several questions:

  • Was revisitation a real and distinct user need?
  • What behaviors should the product support?
  • Should content be organized by source, recency, status, or another model?
  • Would a browser-tab pattern feel familiar or introduce too much complexity?
  • How would people discover and understand the feature inside Facebook?
  • What should happen when tabs became stale, unavailable, or unreliable?

Because the feature did not yet exist, the strategic challenge was to make both the user problem and the product opportunity concrete enough for leadership to fund.

The approach

I moved the work from an ambiguous return problem to a defined product model, usable experience, and funded MVP.

01

I connected Tabs to a broader model of reconsideration and revisitation.

The broader decision-journey work showed that people making more complex decisions often leave and return multiple times before they are ready to act. I distinguished between:

Reconsideration

The thinking and research involved in evaluating options, comparing alternatives, and building confidence.

Revisitation

The actions involved in remembering, organizing, and returning to content encountered earlier.

Tabs primarily addressed revisitation. It gave people a way to preserve open browser context and continue exploring without starting over. This framing made the opportunity more specific than “increase engagement.” It established continuity as a distinct browser job with its own user needs and product implications.

02

I defined the jobs the experience needed to support.

The team needed a clearer basis for evaluating concepts than beginning with the assumption that the answer had to be tabs. I helped articulate the core jobs:

Keep my place

Help me preserve where I was so I do not have to start again.

Continue looking

Help me resume exploration across multiple links or sessions.

Compare options

Help me move among pages without losing context.

Return later

Help me find something again when I am not ready to act now.

Understand what is open

Help me see and manage the content the browser is preserving.

These jobs shaped how we evaluated product models and later informed first-use education, navigation, close behavior, and empty states.

03

I helped compare competing product models.

The team explored several ways to organize return behavior, including:

  • Ad-origin versus organic-origin content
  • Recent versus pinned status
  • Save-for-later and return models
  • A browser-tab model

Each model created different tradeoffs across familiarity, discoverability, scalability, organization, return support, and browser complexity.

I helped shape the case for a tab-based model because it borrowed a familiar browser pattern, supported multiple pages, and could extend beyond a single source or commerce journey. The result was not a literal copy of desktop tabs. It was a mobile, in-app interpretation of continuity that had to coexist with Facebook navigation and browser chrome.

04

I helped define what Tabs should mean before defining every string.

An unfamiliar feature inside Facebook needed more than labels. People needed to understand:

  • What Tabs contained
  • Why the feature was useful
  • How it differed from Facebook navigation
  • Whether pages would remain available
  • How to resume, switch, or close content
  • What would happen when nothing remained open

I explored naming and first-use directions that emphasized different aspects of the value:

  • Continue exploring
  • Return to recently opened links
  • Pick up where you left off
  • See what is still open
  • Manage browser content

The work compared awareness-led, instruction-led, utility-led, and continuity-led approaches. The final framing needed to make the feature understandable without overstating its reliability or teaching unnecessary browser concepts.

05

I co-designed the core flow and the states around it.

The work covered more than opening a tab tray. I helped shape content and behavior across:

  • First-use education
  • Tooltip and entry-point discovery
  • Opening the tab tray
  • Selecting a previous tab
  • Returning to the current page
  • Closing one tab
  • Closing all tabs
  • Confirmation behavior
  • Empty states
  • Overflow-menu entry
  • Unavailable or unreliable states

This made the feature coherent across its full lifecycle, not just its primary interaction.

06

I created implementation-ready content specifications.

The Tabs content system included:

  • Visible strings
  • String descriptions
  • Screen-reader labels
  • Screen-reader descriptions
  • Interaction behavior
  • Accessibility hints
  • Link or destination references
  • Internationalization considerations

This work helped ensure that content intent survived implementation and that nonvisual behavior was defined alongside visible UI. It also created a clearer source of truth across design, engineering, accessibility, and localization.

07

I built the narrative that earned investment.

I authored the leadership narrative connecting:

  • The user problem
  • Decision-journey evidence
  • Jobs-to-be-done
  • Competing product models
  • The proposed Tabs direction
  • Expected user value
  • Roadmap opportunity

The work helped move Tabs from an emerging idea to a funded MVP with additional roadmap commitment. The strategic value was not simply advocating for a feature. It was making an ambiguous opportunity legible enough for leadership to evaluate and invest in.

08

I adapted the launch experience when product reality changed.

Tabs launched on Facebook for Android and exceeded its engagement-rate target. But a technical regression made saved or retained tabs unreliable.

I partnered with design and engineering to reduce feature visibility while the team investigated, rather than continuing to promote behavior the product could not support consistently.

Where the story ends: The regression ultimately outweighed the feature’s value, and Tabs was sunset. That outcome should remain visible in the case study. The work still demonstrated that people engaged with the feature, established continuity as a legitimate browser problem, and created strategic and interaction models that remained relevant after the interface itself disappeared.

Impact

Tabs turned a vague return problem into a funded, launched product direction.

The MVP launched on Facebook for Android and reached a 0.22% engagement rate against a 0.15% goal — 46% above target. The work also:

  • Created leadership alignment around revisitation as a distinct browser need
  • Secured a funded roadmap commitment
  • Translated decision-journey research into a concrete product model
  • Established jobs-to-be-done for browser continuity
  • Produced a complete first-use, navigation, management, and accessibility content system
  • Tested the feature on additional platforms and surfaces
  • Informed later browser thinking about continuity and return journeys

A technical regression ultimately outweighed the feature’s value, and Tabs was sunset. The contribution was defining a real problem, comparing plausible directions, proving early interest, shipping a coherent MVP, responding responsibly when reliability changed, and preserving the useful learning after the feature itself ended.

Reflection

Good product strategy does not guarantee that a feature will survive.

It creates a clearer basis for deciding what to build, how to evaluate it, and what to carry forward when conditions change.

Tabs succeeded in making an ambiguous opportunity concrete enough to fund, build, and test. It also revealed that continuity depends on more than a strong interaction model: the underlying system must preserve state reliably enough for users to trust it.

“I shape strategy by making ambiguous opportunities legible — defining the problem, comparing plausible directions, and building an evidence-based case for where the product should go.”
