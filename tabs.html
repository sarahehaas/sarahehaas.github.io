Closing a critical gap in the browsing journey

People who left Meta’s in-app browser couldn’t easily re-engage with what they had been exploring when they began a new browsing session. The broader re-engagement strategy identified re-visitation as a critical gap and Tabs as the product opportunity to address it.

In collaboration with Product and Design, I defined the Tabs content framework, design principles, naming, value proposition, and first-time user experience for the 0→1 product. I also developed the product narrative for pitch decks and presented it to leadership, building alignment and helping secure roadmap investment.

Problem

Closing the in-app browser ended a user’s active browsing journey. Because many conversion journeys unfolded across multiple browsing sessions, losing that context made it harder for people to re-engage and complete a purchase.

Tabs offered people a lightweight, contextual way to revisit pages they had been actively browsing across in-app browser sessions. (A related product, Link History, required people to opt in and preserved every visited URL in a dense, chronological, undifferentiated list.)

Outcome

The re-engagement strategy identified Tabs as the product to address the re-visitation gap. The product narrative earned leadership alignment and roadmap investment, and Tabs launched in Facebook’s in-app browser and exceeded its engagement goal by 46%.

My role

I defined the content framework, design principles, naming, value proposition, and first-use experience, then developed and presented the product narrative used to build alignment and secure investment.

Key decision

Frame Tabs around active browsing context—not historical link storage—and shape a feasible MVP within the in-app browser’s indirect access constraint.

The work also established continuity as a legitimate browser problem and produced strategic and interaction models that remained useful after the interface itself disappeared.

The challenge

Turning a re-engagement need into a funded 0→1 product

Tabs addressed a critical re-visitation gap by preserving a user’s active browsing context across sessions. The challenge was defining an initial product that worked within the in-app browser’s technical and access constraints, created clear user value, and demonstrated that re-engagement could lead to revenue.

That access constraint made repeat use harder to establish, which also made the revenue opportunity harder to prove. I developed the Tabs product narrative to connect the user need, feasible initial experience, and business case, then presented it to leadership. The narrative earned buy-in and aligned Product, Design, Content, and Engineering around priorities and scope.

What I did

I turned an ambiguous return problem into a product teams could build.

I helped define the user need and jobs-to-be-done, compare product models, and shape the strategic narrative used with leadership.

Then I worked through the actual experience: naming, first-use education, entry points, core flows, edge cases, accessibility-ready content specifications, and launch adaptation.

The final language made the distinction concrete:

Open Tabs

Helped people continue browsing pages that were still open.

Link History

Helped people return to links they had visited previously.

Resolution

Tabs launched as a Facebook Android MVP after earning roadmap investment and leadership alignment around continuity as a distinct browser need.

When a later technical regression made retained tabs unreliable, I partnered with Design and Engineering to reduce feature visibility rather than continue promoting behavior the product could not consistently support.

Where the story ends: The regression ultimately outweighed the feature’s value, and Tabs was sunset.

Reflection

Good product strategy does not guarantee that a feature will survive.

It creates a clearer basis for deciding what to build, how to evaluate it, and what to carry forward when conditions change.
